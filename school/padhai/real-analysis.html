1.1 Continuity of functions of several variables

🔥 High — example question
💡 Concept \(f(x,y)\) do inputs leta hai. Continuity ka matlab: input thoda badle to output bhi thoda hi badle. Ek variable me x sirf left/right se aata hai; do variables me \((x,y)\) kisi bhi raaste se point ke paas aa sakta hai — line, parabola, kuch bhi. Limit tabhi exist karegi jab har raaste par same value aaye.
📖 Definition Let \(E\subseteq\mathbb R^n\) be open and \(f:E\to\mathbb R^m\). f is continuous at \(a\in E\) if for every \(\varepsilon\gt0\) there exists \(\delta\gt0\) such that $$\|x-a\|\lt\delta,\ x\in E\ \Longrightarrow\ \|f(x)-f(a)\|\lt\varepsilon .$$ f is continuous on E if it is continuous at every point of E.
✍️ Solved example Q. Show that \(f(x,y)=\dfrac{xy}{x^2+y^2}\), \((x,y)\ne(0,0)\); \(f(0,0)=0\) is not continuous at (0,0), although \(f_x(0,0)\) and \(f_y(0,0)\) exist.
Solution.
  1. Along the line \(y=mx\): \(f(x,mx)=\dfrac{mx^2}{x^2(1+m^2)}=\dfrac{m}{1+m^2}\).
  2. Hence \(\lim_{x\to0}f(x,mx)=\dfrac{m}{1+m^2}\), which depends on m (m = 0 gives 0, m = 1 gives 1/2).
  3. Different paths give different limits, so \(\lim_{(x,y)\to(0,0)}f\) does not exist. Therefore f is not continuous at (0,0).
  4. \(f_x(0,0)=\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(h,0)-f(0,0)}{h}=\lim\dfrac{0-0}{h}=0\). Similarly \(f_y(0,0)=0\). So both partial derivatives exist. ∎
Remark: existence of partial derivatives does not imply continuity.
🧠 TrickLimit nahi hai dikhana ho → \(y=mx\) ya \(y=mx^2\) rakho. Answer m par depend kare to limit exist nahi karti.

1.2 Partial derivatives

⭐ Medium
📖 Definition $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a,b)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(a+h,b)-f(a,b)}{h},\qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a,b)=\lim_{k\to0}\frac{f(a,b+k)-f(a,b)}{k}$$ provided the limits exist. In general, \(D_jf_i(x)=\lim_{t\to0}\dfrac{f_i(x+te_j)-f_i(x)}{t}\).
💡 Conceptx ke respect me derivative = y ko constant maan kar normal derivative.
✍️ Solved example \(f=x^2y+3y^2\): \(f_x=2xy,\ f_y=x^2+6y,\ f_{xx}=2y,\ f_{xy}=f_{yx}=2x,\ f_{yy}=6\).

1.3 Differentiability — derivative as a linear transformation

🔥 High
💡 ConceptEk variable me \(f(x+h)\approx f(x)+f'(x)h\) — curve ko tangent line se approximate karte hain. Several variables me yahi kaam ek linear map A (matrix) karta hai: \(f(x+h)\approx f(x)+Ah\). Woh matrix = Jacobian \([D_jf_i]\).
📖 Definition Let \(E\subseteq\mathbb R^n\) be open, \(f:E\to\mathbb R^m\), \(x\in E\). If there exists a linear transformation \(A:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m\) such that $$\lim_{h\to0}\frac{\|f(x+h)-f(x)-Ah\|}{\|h\|}=0,$$ then f is differentiable at x and we write \(f'(x)=A\). The matrix of \(f'(x)\) is the Jacobian matrix \(\big[D_jf_i(x)\big]_{m\times n}\).
📜 Theorem 1 (Uniqueness)If f is differentiable at x, the derivative \(f'(x)\) is unique.
✒️ Proof
  1. Suppose \(A_1,A_2\) both satisfy the definition. Put \(B=A_1-A_2\).
  2. \(\|Bh\|\le\|f(x+h)-f(x)-A_2h\|+\|f(x+h)-f(x)-A_1h\|\) (triangle inequality), so \(\dfrac{\|Bh\|}{\|h\|}\to0\) as \(h\to0\).
  3. Fix \(u\ne0\) and put \(h=tu\), \(t\to0\). By linearity \(\dfrac{\|B(tu)\|}{\|tu\|}=\dfrac{\|Bu\|}{\|u\|}\), which does not depend on t.
  4. A quantity independent of t that tends to 0 must be 0. So \(Bu=0\) for every u, i.e. \(B=0\), \(A_1=A_2\). ∎
📜 Theorem 2If f is differentiable at x, then f is continuous at x and all partial derivatives \(D_jf_i(x)\) exist; \(D_jf_i(x)\) is the (i, j) entry of the matrix of \(f'(x)\).
✒️ Proof
  1. \(\|f(x+h)-f(x)\|\le\|f(x+h)-f(x)-Ah\|+\|Ah\|\). The first term tends to 0 (it is small compared with \(\|h\|\)), and \(\|Ah\|\le\|A\|\,\|h\|\to0\). So f is continuous at x.
  2. Put \(h=te_j\): \(\dfrac{f(x+te_j)-f(x)}{t}\to Ae_j\). Taking the i-th component, \(D_jf_i(x)\) exists and equals the (i, j) entry of A. ∎
📜 Theorem 3 (Sufficient condition)If \(f_x,f_y\) exist in a neighbourhood of (a, b) and are continuous at (a, b), then f is differentiable at (a, b).
✒️ Proof (two variables)
  1. Write \(f(a+h,b+k)-f(a,b)=[f(a+h,b+k)-f(a,b+k)]+[f(a,b+k)-f(a,b)]\).
  2. Apply the one-variable Mean Value Theorem to each bracket: first \(=h\,f_x(a+\theta_1h,\,b+k)\), second \(=k\,f_y(a,\,b+\theta_2k)\), \(0\lt\theta_1,\theta_2\lt1\).
  3. By continuity of \(f_x,f_y\) at (a, b): \(f_x(a+\theta_1h,b+k)=f_x(a,b)+\varepsilon_1\), \(f_y(a,b+\theta_2k)=f_y(a,b)+\varepsilon_2\), with \(\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2\to0\) as \((h,k)\to(0,0)\).
  4. So \(f(a+h,b+k)-f(a,b)-[hf_x+kf_y]=h\varepsilon_1+k\varepsilon_2\), and \(\dfrac{|h\varepsilon_1+k\varepsilon_2|}{\sqrt{h^2+k^2}}\le|\varepsilon_1|+|\varepsilon_2|\to0\).
  5. Hence f is differentiable with \(A=(f_x(a,b),\ f_y(a,b))\). ∎
✍️ Solved example — Jacobian \(f(x,y)=(x^2y,\ x+y)\). \(f'(x,y)=\begin{pmatrix}2xy & x^2\\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}\). At (1, 2): \(\begin{pmatrix}4&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\).
🧠 LadderPartials continuous ⇒ Differentiable ⇒ Continuous + partials exist. Ulta nahi.

1.4 Chain rule

⭐ Medium
📜 TheoremLet \(E\subseteq\mathbb R^n\) be open, \(f:E\to\mathbb R^m\) differentiable at \(x_0\), and let g map an open set containing \(f(E)\) into \(\mathbb R^k\), differentiable at \(f(x_0)\). Then \(F=g\circ f\) is differentiable at \(x_0\) and $$F'(x_0)=g'(f(x_0))\,f'(x_0).$$
✒️ Proof
  1. Let \(y_0=f(x_0)\), \(A=f'(x_0)\), \(B=g'(y_0)\). Define \(u(h)=f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)-Ah\), \(v(k)=g(y_0+k)-g(y_0)-Bk\). Then \(\|u(h)\|=\varepsilon(h)\|h\|\), \(\|v(k)\|=\eta(k)\|k\|\) with \(\varepsilon(h)\to0\), \(\eta(k)\to0\).
  2. Put \(k=f(x_0+h)-f(x_0)=Ah+u(h)\). Then \(\|k\|\le[\|A\|+\varepsilon(h)]\|h\|\to0\).
  3. \(F(x_0+h)-F(x_0)-BAh=g(y_0+k)-g(y_0)-BAh=B(k-Ah)+v(k)=Bu(h)+v(k)\).
  4. So \(\dfrac{\|F(x_0+h)-F(x_0)-BAh\|}{\|h\|}\le\|B\|\varepsilon(h)+\eta(k)[\|A\|+\varepsilon(h)]\to0\).
  5. Hence \(F'(x_0)=BA\). ∎
✍️ Solved example \(z=x^2+y^2,\ x=\cos t,\ y=\sin t\): \(\dfrac{dz}{dt}=z_x\dfrac{dx}{dt}+z_y\dfrac{dy}{dt}=2\cos t(-\sin t)+2\sin t\cos t=0\). (Check: z = 1, constant.)

1.5 Taylor's theorem for two variables

🔥 High — statement, proof and expansion
📜 TheoremIf \(f(x,y)\) has continuous partial derivatives of order n in a neighbourhood of (a, b) containing the segment to (a + h, b + k), then $$f(a+h,b+k)=\sum_{r=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{r!}\Big(h\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}+k\tfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\Big)^{r}f(a,b)+R_n,$$ $$R_n=\frac{1}{n!}\Big(h\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}+k\tfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\Big)^{n}f(a+\theta h,\,b+\theta k),\quad 0\lt\theta\lt1.$$
✒️ Proof
  1. Define \(\varphi(t)=f(a+th,\,b+tk)\), \(0\le t\le1\). Then \(\varphi(0)=f(a,b)\), \(\varphi(1)=f(a+h,b+k)\).
  2. By the chain rule, with \(x=a+th,\ y=b+tk\): \(\varphi'(t)=hf_x+kf_y=\Big(h\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}+k\tfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\Big)f\).
  3. Repeating (h, k are constants): \(\varphi^{(r)}(t)=\Big(h\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}+k\tfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\Big)^{r}f(a+th,b+tk)\), continuous for r ≤ n.
  4. By Maclaurin's theorem for one variable: \(\varphi(1)=\varphi(0)+\varphi'(0)+\dfrac{\varphi''(0)}{2!}+\dots+\dfrac{\varphi^{(n-1)}(0)}{(n-1)!}+\dfrac{\varphi^{(n)}(\theta)}{n!}\).
  5. Substituting step 3 gives the theorem. ∎
💡 Second-degree form (numericals ke liye) $$f(a+h,b+k)\approx f+hf_x+kf_y+\tfrac{1}{2!}\big(h^2f_{xx}+2hk\,f_{xy}+k^2f_{yy}\big)$$ (all derivatives at (a, b); expansion about (0,0) me h = x, k = y.)

1.6 Riemann integral — upper and lower sums

🔥 High
💡 ConceptArea ko rectangles se approximate karte hain. Upper sum (sabse oonchi value se rectangle) area se zyada, lower sum (sabse neechi se) kam. Dono ek number par mil jaayein to woh number integral hai.
📖 Definitions A partition of [a, b] is \(P=\{a=x_0\lt x_1\lt\dots\lt x_n=b\}\), \(\Delta x_i=x_i-x_{i-1}\). For bounded f, \(M_i=\sup_{[x_{i-1},x_i]}f,\ m_i=\inf_{[x_{i-1},x_i]}f\), $$U(P,f)=\sum_{i=1}^nM_i\Delta x_i,\qquad L(P,f)=\sum_{i=1}^nm_i\Delta x_i.$$ Upper integral \(\overline{\int_a^b}f=\inf_PU(P,f)\), lower integral \(\underline{\int_a^b}f=\sup_PL(P,f)\). f is Riemann integrable (\(f\in\mathcal R\)) if they are equal; the common value is \(\int_a^bf\,dx\). A partition \(P^*\supseteq P\) is a refinement of P.
📜 Theorem 1 (Refinement)If \(P^*\) is a refinement of P, then \(L(P,f)\le L(P^*,f)\le U(P^*,f)\le U(P,f)\). Consequently \(\underline{\int}f\le\overline{\int}f\).
✒️ Proof
  1. First suppose \(P^*\) has exactly one extra point \(x^*\in(x_{i-1},x_i)\). Let \(w_1=\inf f\) on \([x_{i-1},x^*]\), \(w_2=\inf f\) on \([x^*,x_i]\). Then \(w_1,w_2\ge m_i\).
  2. \(L(P^*,f)-L(P,f)=w_1(x^*-x_{i-1})+w_2(x_i-x^*)-m_i(x_i-x_{i-1})\ge0\).
  3. Repeat for each extra point. Upper sums are handled the same way with sup (they decrease).
  4. For any two partitions \(P_1,P_2\), with \(P^*=P_1\cup P_2\): \(L(P_1)\le L(P^*)\le U(P^*)\le U(P_2)\). So every lower sum ≤ every upper sum; taking sup over \(P_1\) and inf over \(P_2\) gives \(\underline\int f\le\overline\int f\). ∎
📜 Theorem 2 (Riemann's criterion)A bounded function f is Riemann integrable on [a, b] if and only if for every \(\varepsilon\gt0\) there exists a partition P such that \(U(P,f)-L(P,f)\lt\varepsilon\).
✒️ Proof
  1. Sufficiency. For every P: \(L(P)\le\underline\int f\le\overline\int f\le U(P)\). So \(0\le\overline\int f-\underline\int f\le U(P)-L(P)\lt\varepsilon\). Since ε is arbitrary, \(\overline\int f=\underline\int f\), i.e. \(f\in\mathcal R\).
  2. Necessity. Let \(f\in\mathcal R\), \(I=\int f\). By definition of inf and sup there are partitions \(P_1,P_2\) with \(U(P_2)\lt I+\frac\varepsilon2\) and \(L(P_1)\gt I-\frac\varepsilon2\).
  3. Let \(P=P_1\cup P_2\). By Theorem 1, \(U(P)\le U(P_2)\lt I+\frac\varepsilon2\lt L(P_1)+\varepsilon\le L(P)+\varepsilon\).
  4. So \(U(P)-L(P)\lt\varepsilon\). ∎
📜 Theorem 3(a) Every continuous function on [a, b] is Riemann integrable. (b) Every monotonic function on [a, b] is Riemann integrable.
✒️ Proof (a) f is uniformly continuous on the compact set [a, b]. Given ε > 0, choose δ > 0 with \(|x-t|\lt\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(t)|\lt\frac{\varepsilon}{b-a}\). Take P with every \(\Delta x_i\lt\delta\). Then \(M_i-m_i\le\frac{\varepsilon}{b-a}\) and \(U-L=\sum(M_i-m_i)\Delta x_i\le\frac{\varepsilon}{b-a}(b-a)=\varepsilon\). By Riemann's criterion, \(f\in\mathcal R\).
(b) Let f be increasing. Take P with n equal parts, \(\Delta x_i=\frac{b-a}{n}\). Then \(M_i=f(x_i),\ m_i=f(x_{i-1})\), so \(U-L=\frac{b-a}{n}\sum[f(x_i)-f(x_{i-1})]=\frac{b-a}{n}[f(b)-f(a)]\lt\varepsilon\) for n large. ∎
✍️ Solved example 1 — evaluate by definition Q. Show that \(f(x)=x\) is integrable on [0, 1] and \(\int_0^1x\,dx=\frac12\).
Take \(P_n=\{0,\frac1n,\dots,1\}\). \(U(P_n)=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac in\cdot\frac1n=\frac{n(n+1)}{2n^2}=\frac{n+1}{2n}\), \(L(P_n)=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{i-1}{n}\cdot\frac1n=\frac{n-1}{2n}\). \(U-L=\frac1n\to0\), so f is integrable; and \(\frac{n-1}{2n}\le\int\le\frac{n+1}{2n}\) for all n gives \(\int_0^1x\,dx=\frac12\). ∎
✍️ Solved example 2 — non-integrable function Q. Show that \(f(x)=1\) (x rational), \(0\) (x irrational) is not Riemann integrable on [0, 1].
Every subinterval contains both rationals and irrationals (density). So for every P, \(M_i=1,\ m_i=0\), giving \(U(P,f)=1\), \(L(P,f)=0\). Hence \(\overline\int f=1\ne0=\underline\int f\). f is not integrable. ∎

1.7 Properties of the Riemann integral

⭐ Medium
📜 TheoremIf \(f,g\in\mathcal R\) on [a, b] and c is a constant, then:
  1. \(f+g\in\mathcal R\) and \(\int(f+g)=\int f+\int g\); \(cf\in\mathcal R\), \(\int cf=c\int f\).
  2. If \(f\le g\), then \(\int f\le\int g\).
  3. If \(a\lt c\lt b\), then \(\int_a^bf=\int_a^cf+\int_c^bf\).
  4. If \(|f|\le M\), then \(\big|\int_a^bf\big|\le M(b-a)\).
  5. \(|f|\in\mathcal R\) and \(\big|\int f\big|\le\int|f|\); \(fg\in\mathcal R\).
✒️ Proof of (1) and (5) (1) On any subinterval, \(\sup(f+g)\le\sup f+\sup g\) and \(\inf(f+g)\ge\inf f+\inf g\). So \(L(P,f)+L(P,g)\le L(P,f+g)\le U(P,f+g)\le U(P,f)+U(P,g)\). Choose P (a common refinement) with \(U-L\lt\frac\varepsilon2\) for both f and g; then \(U(P,f+g)-L(P,f+g)\lt\varepsilon\), so \(f+g\in\mathcal R\), and the inequalities give \(\int(f+g)=\int f+\int g\).
(5) On any subinterval, \(\big||f(x)|-|f(t)|\big|\le|f(x)-f(t)|\), so \(\sup|f|-\inf|f|\le M_i-m_i\). Hence \(U(P,|f|)-L(P,|f|)\le U(P,f)-L(P,f)\lt\varepsilon\), so \(|f|\in\mathcal R\). Since \(-|f|\le f\le|f|\), property (2) gives \(|\int f|\le\int|f|\). For fg use \(fg=\frac14[(f+g)^2-(f-g)^2]\) and the fact that \(\varphi(u)=u^2\) (continuous) of an integrable function is integrable. ∎

1.8 Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

🔥 High — 10-mark proof
💡 ConceptIntegration aur differentiation ek doosre ke ulte hain. \(F(x)=\int_a^xf\) = area function; uska derivative = f. Aur \(\int_a^bf=F(b)-F(a)\).
📜 Theorem 1 (First FTC)Let \(f\in\mathcal R\) on [a, b] and \(F(x)=\int_a^xf(t)\,dt\). Then F is continuous on [a, b]. Furthermore, if f is continuous at \(x_0\in[a,b]\), then F is differentiable at \(x_0\) and \(F'(x_0)=f(x_0)\).
✒️ Proof
  1. f is bounded: \(|f|\le M\). For \(x\lt y\): \(|F(y)-F(x)|=\big|\int_x^yf\big|\le M(y-x)\). So \(|y-x|\lt\varepsilon/M\Rightarrow|F(y)-F(x)|\lt\varepsilon\): F is (uniformly) continuous.
  2. Let f be continuous at \(x_0\). Given ε > 0 choose δ with \(|t-x_0|\lt\delta\Rightarrow|f(t)-f(x_0)|\lt\varepsilon\).
  3. For \(0\lt|h|\lt\delta\): \(\dfrac{F(x_0+h)-F(x_0)}{h}-f(x_0)=\dfrac1h\int_{x_0}^{x_0+h}[f(t)-f(x_0)]\,dt\).
  4. Its absolute value is \(\le\dfrac1{|h|}\cdot\varepsilon|h|=\varepsilon\). Hence \(F'(x_0)=f(x_0)\). ∎
📜 Theorem 2 (Second FTC)If \(f\in\mathcal R\) on [a, b] and there is a differentiable function F on [a, b] with \(F'=f\), then \(\displaystyle\int_a^bf(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)\).
✒️ Proof
  1. Let ε > 0. By Riemann's criterion choose P with \(U(P,f)-L(P,f)\lt\varepsilon\).
  2. By the Mean Value Theorem, for each i there is \(t_i\in(x_{i-1},x_i)\) with \(F(x_i)-F(x_{i-1})=f(t_i)\Delta x_i\).
  3. Summing over i, the left side telescopes: \(\sum_{i=1}^nf(t_i)\Delta x_i=F(b)-F(a)\).
  4. Since \(m_i\le f(t_i)\le M_i\): \(L(P,f)\le F(b)-F(a)\le U(P,f)\). Also \(L(P,f)\le\int_a^bf\le U(P,f)\).
  5. Both numbers lie in an interval of length \(\lt\varepsilon\): \(\big|F(b)-F(a)-\int_a^bf\big|\lt\varepsilon\). As ε is arbitrary, equality holds. ∎
✍️ Solved example\(F(x)=\int_0^{x^2}\sin t\,dt\). By FTC and the chain rule, \(F'(x)=2x\sin(x^2)\).

Unit II — Riemann–Stieltjes Integral

Definition, existence, properties, limit of sums, integration and differentiation, mean value theorems.

2.1 Definition of the R–S integral

🔥 High
💡 ConceptRiemann integral me har rectangle ki width \(\Delta x_i\) thi. R–S integral me width ki jagah ek increasing function α ka badlav \(\Delta\alpha_i\) aata hai. Example: f = daam, α = kitna maal bika ⇒ \(\int f\,d\alpha\) = total kamai. \(\alpha(x)=x\) rakhne par yeh Riemann integral ban jaata hai.
📖 Definition Let α be monotonically increasing on [a, b] and f bounded on [a, b]. For a partition P put \(\Delta\alpha_i=\alpha(x_i)-\alpha(x_{i-1})\ (\ge0)\), $$U(P,f,\alpha)=\sum_{i=1}^nM_i\,\Delta\alpha_i,\qquad L(P,f,\alpha)=\sum_{i=1}^nm_i\,\Delta\alpha_i .$$ If \(\inf_PU(P,f,\alpha)=\sup_PL(P,f,\alpha)\), f is said to be Riemann–Stieltjes integrable with respect to α, written \(f\in\mathcal R(\alpha)\), and the common value is denoted \(\int_a^bf\,d\alpha\).
💡 NoteRefinement theorem (1.6 Theorem 1) R–S ke liye bhi bilkul waise hi sach hai — proof me har jagah \(\Delta x_i\) ki jagah \(\Delta\alpha_i\) likhiye.

2.2 Existence — Riemann's condition and integrable classes

🔥 High — 10-mark proof
📜 Theorem 1 (Riemann's condition)\(f\in\mathcal R(\alpha)\) on [a, b] if and only if for every \(\varepsilon\gt0\) there exists a partition P such that \(U(P,f,\alpha)-L(P,f,\alpha)\lt\varepsilon\).
✒️ Proof
  1. (⇐) \(L(P,f,\alpha)\le\underline\int f\,d\alpha\le\overline\int f\,d\alpha\le U(P,f,\alpha)\). Hence \(0\le\overline\int-\underline\int\lt\varepsilon\) for every ε, so they are equal.
  2. (⇒) Let \(f\in\mathcal R(\alpha)\) with value I. Choose \(P_1,P_2\) with \(U(P_2)-I\lt\frac\varepsilon2\), \(I-L(P_1)\lt\frac\varepsilon2\). For \(P=P_1\cup P_2\): \(U(P)\le U(P_2)\lt I+\frac\varepsilon2\lt L(P_1)+\varepsilon\le L(P)+\varepsilon\). ∎
📜 Theorem 2(a) If f is continuous on [a, b], then \(f\in\mathcal R(\alpha)\).
(b) If f is monotonic on [a, b] and α is continuous on [a, b], then \(f\in\mathcal R(\alpha)\).
(c) If f is bounded with finitely many discontinuities on [a, b], and α is continuous at each of them, then \(f\in\mathcal R(\alpha)\).
✒️ Proof of (a)
  1. Let ε > 0. Choose η > 0 with \([\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)]\eta\lt\varepsilon\).
  2. f is uniformly continuous on [a, b]: there is δ > 0 with \(|x-t|\lt\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(t)|\lt\eta\).
  3. Take P with \(\Delta x_i\lt\delta\) for all i. Then \(M_i-m_i\le\eta\).
  4. \(U(P,f,\alpha)-L(P,f,\alpha)=\sum(M_i-m_i)\Delta\alpha_i\le\eta\sum\Delta\alpha_i=\eta[\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)]\lt\varepsilon\). By Theorem 1, \(f\in\mathcal R(\alpha)\). ∎
✒️ Proof of (b)
  1. Let f be increasing (decreasing is similar). Given ε, choose n. Since α is continuous, by the intermediate value theorem we can choose P with \(\Delta\alpha_i=\frac{\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)}{n}\) for every i.
  2. Then \(M_i=f(x_i),\ m_i=f(x_{i-1})\), and \(U-L=\frac{\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)}{n}\sum[f(x_i)-f(x_{i-1})]=\frac{\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)}{n}[f(b)-f(a)]\).
  3. This is \(\lt\varepsilon\) for n large. ∎
✒️ Proof of (c)Let \(|f|\le M\) and let E be the (finite) set of discontinuities. Since α is continuous at each point of E, cover E by finitely many disjoint intervals \([u_j,v_j]\) with \(\sum[\alpha(v_j)-\alpha(u_j)]\lt\varepsilon\). On the remaining set K (a finite union of closed intervals) f is continuous, hence uniformly continuous: choose δ so that \(|f(s)-f(t)|\lt\varepsilon\) for \(s,t\in K,\ |s-t|\lt\delta\). Form P from all \(u_j,v_j\) and further points of K with mesh \(\lt\delta\) (no point inside any \((u_j,v_j)\)). Then \(M_i-m_i\le2M\) on the covering intervals and \(\le\varepsilon\) on the rest, so \(U-L\le2M\varepsilon+\varepsilon[\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)]\), which is arbitrarily small. By Theorem 1, \(f\in\mathcal R(\alpha)\). ∎

⭐ Medium

2.4 Reduction to the Riemann integral; step functions

🔥 High — numericals
📜 Theorem 1Let α be increasing and \(\alpha'\in\mathcal R\) on [a, b]. Let f be bounded. Then \(f\in\mathcal R(\alpha)\iff f\alpha'\in\mathcal R\), and in that case $$\int_a^bf\,d\alpha=\int_a^bf(x)\,\alpha'(x)\,dx .$$
✒️ Proof
  1. Let ε > 0 and \(|f|\le M\). Since \(\alpha'\in\mathcal R\), choose P with \(U(P,\alpha')-L(P,\alpha')\lt\varepsilon\).
  2. By the MVT, \(\Delta\alpha_i=\alpha'(t_i)\Delta x_i\) for some \(t_i\in(x_{i-1},x_i)\). For any \(s_i\) in the same subinterval, \(\sum|\alpha'(s_i)-\alpha'(t_i)|\Delta x_i\le U(P,\alpha')-L(P,\alpha')\lt\varepsilon\).
  3. So \(\big|\sum f(s_i)\Delta\alpha_i-\sum f(s_i)\alpha'(s_i)\Delta x_i\big|\le M\varepsilon\).
  4. Hence \(\sum f(s_i)\Delta\alpha_i\le U(P,f\alpha')+M\varepsilon\) for every choice of \(s_i\); taking sup, \(U(P,f,\alpha)\le U(P,f\alpha')+M\varepsilon\). Similarly \(U(P,f\alpha')\le U(P,f,\alpha)+M\varepsilon\). So \(|U(P,f,\alpha)-U(P,f\alpha')|\le M\varepsilon\).
  5. This also holds for every refinement of P, so \(|\overline\int f\,d\alpha-\overline\int f\alpha'dx|\le M\varepsilon\); ε arbitrary ⇒ the upper integrals are equal. The same argument works for lower integrals. Hence the result. ∎
📖 Unit step function\(I(x)=0\) for \(x\le0\), \(I(x)=1\) for \(x\gt0\).
📜 Theorem 2If \(a\lt s\lt b\), f is bounded on [a, b] and continuous at s, and \(\alpha(x)=I(x-s)\), then \(\int_a^bf\,d\alpha=f(s)\). More generally, if \(c_n\ge0\), \(\sum c_n\) converges, \(\{s_n\}\) are distinct points of (a, b), \(\alpha(x)=\sum c_nI(x-s_n)\) and f is continuous on [a, b], then \(\int_a^bf\,d\alpha=\sum c_nf(s_n)\).
✒️ Proof (single jump)Take \(P=\{x_0=a,\ x_1=s,\ x_2,\ x_3=b\}\) with \(s\lt x_2\lt b\). Then \(\Delta\alpha_1=0,\ \Delta\alpha_2=1,\ \Delta\alpha_3=0\). So \(U(P)=M_2,\ L(P)=m_2\), the sup and inf of f on \([s,x_2]\). As \(x_2\to s\), continuity at s gives \(M_2,m_2\to f(s)\). Hence \(\int f\,d\alpha=f(s)\). ∎

✒️ Proof

2.6 Mean value theorems for R–S integrals

🔥 High
📜 First Mean Value TheoremIf f is continuous on [a, b] and α is increasing, there exists \(\xi\in[a,b]\) such that $$\int_a^bf\,d\alpha=f(\xi)\,[\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)].$$
✒️ Proof
  1. f attains its minimum m and maximum M on [a, b]. Since \(m\le f\le M\), property (2) gives \(m[\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)]\le\int f\,d\alpha\le M[\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)]\).
  2. If \(\alpha(b)=\alpha(a)\), both sides are 0 and any ξ works. Otherwise \(\lambda=\dfrac{\int f\,d\alpha}{\alpha(b)-\alpha(a)}\) satisfies \(m\le\lambda\le M\).
  3. By the intermediate value theorem, \(\lambda=f(\xi)\) for some \(\xi\in[a,b]\). ∎
📜 Second Mean Value TheoremIf α is continuous and f is monotonic on [a, b], there exists \(\xi\in[a,b]\) such that $$\int_a^bf\,d\alpha=f(a)[\alpha(\xi)-\alpha(a)]+f(b)[\alpha(b)-\alpha(\xi)].$$
✒️ Proof
  1. By 2.2(b), \(f\in\mathcal R(\alpha)\); by integration by parts, \(\alpha\in\mathcal R(f)\) and \(\int_a^bf\,d\alpha=f(b)\alpha(b)-f(a)\alpha(a)-\int_a^b\alpha\,df\).
  2. Let f be increasing (if decreasing, apply the argument to −f). α is continuous, so by the First MVT with the roles of f and α exchanged: \(\int_a^b\alpha\,df=\alpha(\xi)[f(b)-f(a)]\) for some ξ.
  3. Substitute: \(\int f\,d\alpha=f(b)\alpha(b)-f(a)\alpha(a)-\alpha(\xi)f(b)+\alpha(\xi)f(a)=f(a)[\alpha(\xi)-\alpha(a)]+f(b)[\alpha(b)-\alpha(\xi)]\). ∎

3.1 Countable and uncountable sets

🔥 High
💡 ConceptCountable = jiske elements ko 1, 2, 3, … se gin sakein, koi chhoote nahi. Even numbers (n → 2n) aur integers (0, 1, −1, 2, −2, …) dono countable hain.
📖 Definitions
  • A ~ B (A equivalent to B) if there is a one-one mapping of A onto B.
  • A is finite if A ~ {1, …, n} for some n (or A = ∅); countable (denumerable) if A ~ ℕ; at most countable if finite or countable; uncountable otherwise.
📜 Theorem 1Every infinite subset of a countable set is countable.
✒️ Proof
  1. Let A be countable, arranged as \(x_1,x_2,x_3,\dots\) (distinct), and E ⊆ A infinite.
  2. Let \(n_1\) be the smallest index with \(x_{n_1}\in E\); having chosen \(n_1,\dots,n_{k-1}\), let \(n_k\) be the smallest index \(\gt n_{k-1}\) with \(x_{n_k}\in E\) (it exists because E is infinite).
  3. Then \(k\mapsto x_{n_k}\) is one-one from ℕ onto E (every element of E is some \(x_m\), and it is picked when the index m is reached). So E is countable. ∎
📜 Theorem 2The union of a countable family of countable sets is countable.
✒️ Proof
  1. Let \(E_n=\{x_{n1},x_{n2},x_{n3},\dots\}\), n = 1, 2, …. Write them in an infinite array: row n lists \(E_n\).
  2. Arrange along diagonals: \(x_{11};\ x_{21},x_{12};\ x_{31},x_{22},x_{13};\dots\) (diagonal k contains the elements with i + j = k + 1 — finitely many).
  3. This lists every element of \(S=\bigcup E_n\). Removing repetitions, S is an infinite subset of a countable list, hence countable by Theorem 1 (S is infinite since it contains \(E_1\)). ∎
📜 Theorem 3ℚ is countable.
✒️ Proof
  1. For each q ∈ ℕ, \(E_q=\{p/q:p\in\mathbb Z\}\) is countable (it is equivalent to ℤ via p/q ↦ p).
  2. \(\mathbb Q=\bigcup_{q=1}^\infty E_q\) is a countable union of countable sets, hence countable by Theorem 2. ∎
Alternative (height method): height of p/q (lowest terms, q > 0) = |p| + q. Each height has finitely many rationals; list them height by height.
📜 Theorem 4 (Cantor)The interval (0, 1), and hence ℝ, is uncountable.
✒️ Proof (diagonal method)
  1. Suppose (0, 1) is countable: \(x_1,x_2,x_3,\dots\). Write each in decimal form \(x_n=0.a_{n1}a_{n2}a_{n3}\ldots\), using the expansion that does not end in recurring 9s (so it is unique).
  2. Define \(y=0.b_1b_2b_3\ldots\) where \(b_n=5\) if \(a_{nn}\ne5\), and \(b_n=4\) if \(a_{nn}=5\).
  3. Then \(y\in(0,1)\), its expansion has no 0s or 9s (so it is its unique expansion), and \(b_n\ne a_{nn}\) for every n. Hence \(y\ne x_n\) for all n.
  4. This contradicts that the list contains all of (0, 1). So (0, 1) is uncountable; since (0, 1) ⊂ ℝ, ℝ is uncountable (by Theorem 1). ∎

📜 Cantor's theoremFor every set A, card A < card P(A), where P(A) is the power set of A.
✒️ Proof
  1. \(a\mapsto\{a\}\) is one-one from A into P(A), so card A ≤ card P(A).
  2. Suppose \(f:A\to P(A)\) is onto. Let \(B=\{a\in A:a\notin f(a)\}\in P(A)\). Then \(B=f(b)\) for some b ∈ A.
  3. If b ∈ B, then by definition b ∉ f(b) = B — contradiction. If b ∉ B = f(b), then by definition b ∈ B — contradiction.
  4. So no onto map exists; A is not equivalent to P(A). Hence card A < card P(A). ∎

3.3 Schroeder–Bernstein theorem

🔥 High — full proof
💡 ConceptA ko B me one-one fit kar sakein aur B ko A me bhi — to A aur B ka size barabar. Jaise a ≤ b aur b ≤ a ⇒ a = b.
📜 TheoremIf A is equivalent to a subset of B and B is equivalent to a subset of A, then A is equivalent to B. Equivalently: card A ≤ card B and card B ≤ card A ⇒ card A = card B.
✒️ Proof
  1. Let \(f:A\to B\) and \(g:B\to A\) be one-one. We construct a one-one onto map \(h:A\to B\).
  2. Define \(A_0=A\setminus g(B)\) (points of A not hit by g), and recursively \(A_{n+1}=g(f(A_n))\). Put \(A^*=\bigcup_{n\ge0}A_n\).
  3. Define \(h(x)=f(x)\) if \(x\in A^*\), and \(h(x)=g^{-1}(x)\) if \(x\notin A^*\). This makes sense: if \(x\notin A^*\) then \(x\notin A_0\), so \(x\in g(B)\), and \(g^{-1}(x)\) is a unique point (g is one-one).
  4. h is one-one. f and \(g^{-1}\) are each one-one. Suppose \(f(x)=g^{-1}(y)\) with \(x\in A^*\) (say \(x\in A_n\)) and \(y\notin A^*\). Then \(y=g(f(x))\in g(f(A_n))=A_{n+1}\subseteq A^*\) — contradiction.
  5. h is onto. Let b ∈ B. If \(b\in f(A^*)\), then b = h(x) for some x ∈ A*. Otherwise put \(y=g(b)\). If y were in A*, then y ∉ A₀ (since y ∈ g(B)), so \(y\in A_{n+1}=g(f(A_n))\) for some n; as g is one-one, \(b\in f(A_n)\subseteq f(A^*)\) — contradiction. So \(y\notin A^*\) and \(h(y)=g^{-1}(y)=b\).
  6. Thus h is a bijection and A ~ B. ∎
✍️ Solved example Q. Show that (0, 1) ~ [0, 1].
\(f:(0,1)\to[0,1]\), \(f(x)=x\) is one-one. \(g:[0,1]\to(0,1)\), \(g(x)=\frac{x+1}{3}\) is one-one (its image is [1/3, 2/3] ⊂ (0, 1)). By the Schroeder–Bernstein theorem, (0, 1) ~ [0, 1]. ∎

3.4 Series of arbitrary terms — convergence, divergence, oscillation

⭐ Medium
📖 DefinitionsLet \(s_n=a_1+\dots+a_n\). The series \(\sum a_n\) converges to s if \(s_n\to s\) (finite); diverges if \(s_n\to+\infty\) or \(-\infty\); oscillates finitely if \(\{s_n\}\) is bounded but not convergent (e.g. \(\sum(-1)^{n}\)); oscillates infinitely if \(\{s_n\}\) is unbounded and does not tend to ±∞ (e.g. \(\sum(-1)^nn\)).
📜 Theorem (Cauchy criterion)\(\sum a_n\) converges iff for every ε > 0 there exists N such that \(|a_{n+1}+a_{n+2}+\dots+a_m|\lt\varepsilon\) for all \(m\gt n\ge N\).
✒️ Proof\(a_{n+1}+\dots+a_m=s_m-s_n\). The condition says exactly that \(\{s_n\}\) is a Cauchy sequence, and in ℝ a sequence converges iff it is Cauchy. ∎
Corollary. If \(\sum a_n\) converges then \(a_n\to0\) (take m = n + 1). The converse is false: \(\sum\frac1n\) diverges though \(\frac1n\to0\).

📜 Theorem 2 (Leibniz test)If \(u_n\ge u_{n+1}\gt0\) for all n and \(u_n\to0\), then \(\sum(-1)^{n+1}u_n\) converges.
✒️ Proof

3.6 Abel's and Dirichlet's tests

🔥 High — proof + application
📜 Abel's lemma (partial summation)If \(A_n=a_1+\dots+a_n\) (\(A_0=0\)), then $$\sum_{k=1}^na_kb_k=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}A_k(b_k-b_{k+1})+A_nb_n .$$
✒️ ProofPut \(a_k=A_k-A_{k-1}\): \(\sum_{k=1}^n(A_k-A_{k-1})b_k=\sum_{k=1}^nA_kb_k-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}A_kb_{k+1}=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}A_k(b_k-b_{k+1})+A_nb_n\). ∎
📜 Dirichlet's testIf the partial sums \(A_n\) of \(\sum a_n\) are bounded and \(\{b_n\}\) is monotonically decreasing with \(b_n\to0\), then \(\sum a_nb_n\) converges.
✒️ Proof
  1. Let \(|A_n|\le M\) for all n. For \(m\gt n\), partial summation on the block from n + 1 to m gives \(\sum_{k=n+1}^ma_kb_k=\sum_{k=n+1}^{m-1}A_k(b_k-b_{k+1})+A_mb_m-A_nb_{n+1}\).
  2. Since \(b_k-b_{k+1}\ge0\): \(\big|\sum_{k=n+1}^ma_kb_k\big|\le M\sum_{k=n+1}^{m-1}(b_k-b_{k+1})+Mb_m+Mb_{n+1}=M(b_{n+1}-b_m)+Mb_m+Mb_{n+1}=2Mb_{n+1}\).
  3. As \(b_n\to0\), choose N with \(2Mb_{N+1}\lt\varepsilon\); then the block is \(\lt\varepsilon\) for \(m\gt n\ge N\). By the Cauchy criterion \(\sum a_nb_n\) converges. ∎
📜 Abel's testIf \(\sum a_n\) converges and \(\{b_n\}\) is monotonic and bounded, then \(\sum a_nb_n\) converges.
✒️ Proof
  1. A bounded monotonic sequence converges: \(b_n\to b\). If \(\{b_n\}\) is decreasing, \(c_n=b_n-b\) decreases to 0; if increasing, \(c_n=b-b_n\) decreases to 0.
  2. \(\sum a_n\) converges, so its partial sums are bounded. By Dirichlet's test, \(\sum a_nc_n\) converges.
  3. \(\sum a_nb_n=\pm\sum a_nc_n+b\sum a_n\), a combination of two convergent series. ∎
✍️ Solved example 1 Q. Show that \(\sum\frac{\sin n\theta}{n}\) converges for every real θ.
If θ = 2kπ every term is 0. Otherwise \(\sin\frac\theta2\ne0\) and, using \(2\sin\frac\theta2\sin k\theta=\cos(k-\frac12)\theta-\cos(k+\frac12)\theta\) and telescoping, \(\sum_{k=1}^n\sin k\theta=\dfrac{\cos\frac\theta2-\cos(n+\frac12)\theta}{2\sin\frac\theta2}\), so \(\big|\sum_{k=1}^n\sin k\theta\big|\le\dfrac{1}{|\sin\frac\theta2|}\) — bounded. \(b_n=\frac1n\) decreases to 0. By Dirichlet's test the series converges. ∎
✍️ Solved example 2 Q. Show that \(\sum\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\left(1+\frac1n\right)^n\) converges.
\(\sum\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\) converges (Leibniz). \(b_n=(1+\frac1n)^n\) is increasing and bounded by e. By Abel's test the series converges. ∎
🧠 Yaad rakhne ki trickDirichlet: \(\sum a_n\) sirf bounded (kamzor) ⇒ \(b_n\to0\) chahiye (mazboot). Abel: \(\sum a_n\) convergent (mazboot) ⇒ \(b_n\) sirf bounded (kamzor) chalega.

3.7 Multiplication of series (Cauchy product)

⭐ Medium
📖 DefinitionThe Cauchy product of \(\sum_{n\ge0}a_n\) and \(\sum_{n\ge0}b_n\) is \(\sum c_n\), \(c_n=\sum_{k=0}^na_kb_{n-k}\).
📜 Mertens' theoremIf \(\sum a_n=A\) converges absolutely and \(\sum b_n=B\) converges, then \(\sum c_n\) converges to AB.
✒️ Proof
  1. Let \(A_n,B_n,C_n\) be the partial sums (0 to n), and \(\beta_n=B_n-B\to0\).
  2. Regrouping the finite sum: \(C_n=a_0B_n+a_1B_{n-1}+\dots+a_nB_0=A_nB+\gamma_n\), where \(\gamma_n=a_0\beta_n+a_1\beta_{n-1}+\dots+a_n\beta_0\).
  3. Since \(A_nB\to AB\), it suffices to show \(\gamma_n\to0\). Let \(\alpha=\sum|a_n|\). Given ε, choose N with \(|\beta_k|\le\varepsilon\) for k ≥ N.
  4. \(|\gamma_n|\le|\beta_0a_n+\dots+\beta_Na_{n-N}|+|\beta_{N+1}a_{n-N-1}+\dots+\beta_na_0|\le|\beta_0a_n+\dots+\beta_Na_{n-N}|+\varepsilon\alpha\).
  5. With N fixed, \(a_k\to0\) makes the first term \(\to0\) as \(n\to\infty\). So \(\limsup|\gamma_n|\le\varepsilon\alpha\); ε arbitrary ⇒ \(\gamma_n\to0\). ∎
📜 Abel's theorem (on products)If \(\sum a_n=A\), \(\sum b_n=B\) and their Cauchy product \(\sum c_n=C\) all converge, then C = AB.
✍️ Example — product of conditionally convergent series may diverge \(a_n=b_n=\frac{(-1)^n}{\sqrt{n+1}}\) (n ≥ 0) converge (Leibniz). \(|c_n|=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{1}{\sqrt{(k+1)(n-k+1)}}\ge\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{2}{n+2}=\frac{2(n+1)}{n+2}\) (AM–GM: \(\sqrt{(k+1)(n-k+1)}\le\frac{n+2}{2}\)). So \(c_n\not\to0\) and \(\sum c_n\) diverges.

3.8 Rearrangement of terms; Riemann's theorem

🔥 High
📖 DefinitionIf \(k:\mathbb N\to\mathbb N\) is one-one and onto, \(\sum a_{k(n)}\) is a rearrangement of \(\sum a_n\).
📜 Theorem 1 (Dirichlet)If \(\sum a_n\) converges absolutely to s, every rearrangement converges to s.
✒️ Proof
  1. Let \(s'_n\) be the partial sums of \(\sum a_{k(n)}\). Given ε, choose N with \(\sum_{i=N+1}^m|a_i|\lt\varepsilon\) for all m > N (Cauchy criterion).
  2. Choose p so large that \(\{1,\dots,N\}\subseteq\{k(1),\dots,k(p)\}\).
  3. For n > max(p, N), in \(s_n-s'_n\) the terms \(a_1,\dots,a_N\) cancel; every remaining term has index > N, so \(|s_n-s'_n|\le\sum_{N\lt i\le m}|a_i|\lt\varepsilon\) for a suitable m.
  4. Hence \(s'_n-s_n\to0\) and \(s'_n\to s\). ∎
📜 Theorem 2 (Riemann's rearrangement theorem)Let \(\sum a_n\) be conditionally convergent. Given \(-\infty\le\alpha\le\beta\le\infty\), there is a rearrangement whose partial sums satisfy \(\liminf s'_n=\alpha,\ \limsup s'_n=\beta\). In particular, for any real S there is a rearrangement converging to S, and rearrangements diverging to ±∞.
✒️ Proof (for a real target S)
  1. Let \(p_n\) be the positive terms and \(q_n\) the absolute values of the negative terms, in their original order.
  2. Both \(\sum p_n\) and \(\sum q_n\) diverge: if both converged, \(\sum|a_n|\) would converge; if exactly one converged, \(\sum a_n\) would diverge. Also \(p_n,q_n\to0\) since \(a_n\to0\).
  3. Take just enough p's so that the sum first exceeds S; then just enough −q's so that the sum first goes below S; then p's again to exceed S; and so on. This is always possible because what remains of \(\sum p_n\) and \(\sum q_n\) is still infinite.
  4. Every term is used exactly once, so this is a rearrangement.
  5. At each switch the partial sum differs from S by at most the last term used, and between switches it lies between such values. Since the terms used tend to 0, \(s'_n\to S\). ∎
✍️ Example \(1-\frac12+\frac13-\frac14+\dots=\log2\). Rearranged as two positive, one negative: \((1+\frac13-\frac12)+(\frac15+\frac17-\frac14)+\dots=\frac32\log2\). Same terms, different sum.

4.1 Pointwise and uniform convergence

🔥 High
💡 ConceptPointwise: har point x par \(f_n(x)\to f(x)\), lekin N har x ke liye alag ho sakta hai. Uniform: ek hi N saare x ke liye ek saath kaam kare — limit f ke graph ke chaaron taraf ε-patti banao, to N ke baad har \(f_n\) ka poora graph patti ke andar.
📖 Definitions Pointwise: \(\{f_n\}\) converges pointwise to f on E if for each x ∈ E and ε > 0 there exists \(N=N(\varepsilon,x)\) with \(|f_n(x)-f(x)|\lt\varepsilon\) for all n ≥ N.
Uniform: \(\{f_n\}\) converges uniformly to f on E if for each ε > 0 there exists \(N=N(\varepsilon)\), independent of x, with \(|f_n(x)-f(x)|\lt\varepsilon\) for all n ≥ N and all x ∈ E.
A series \(\sum u_n(x)\) converges uniformly if its partial sums \(S_n(x)\) do.
💡 NoteUniform ⇒ pointwise. Converse false (\(x^n\) on [0, 1], 4.3 dekhiye).

4.2 Cauchy criterion for uniform convergence

🔥 High — proof
📜 Theorem\(\{f_n\}\) converges uniformly on E iff for every ε > 0 there exists N such that \(|f_n(x)-f_m(x)|\lt\varepsilon\) for all m, n ≥ N and all x ∈ E.
✒️ Proof
  1. Necessity. Let \(f_n\to f\) uniformly. Choose N with \(|f_n(x)-f(x)|\lt\frac\varepsilon2\) for n ≥ N, x ∈ E. Then for m, n ≥ N: \(|f_n(x)-f_m(x)|\le|f_n(x)-f(x)|+|f(x)-f_m(x)|\lt\varepsilon\).
  2. Sufficiency. For each fixed x, \(\{f_n(x)\}\) is a Cauchy sequence of reals, so it converges; call the limit f(x).
  3. In \(|f_n(x)-f_m(x)|\lt\varepsilon\) (n ≥ N fixed), let \(m\to\infty\): \(|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le\varepsilon\) for all n ≥ N and all x ∈ E.
  4. N does not depend on x, so \(f_n\to f\) uniformly. ∎
Series form: \(\sum u_n\) converges uniformly on E iff \(|u_{n+1}(x)+\dots+u_m(x)|\lt\varepsilon\) for all m > n ≥ N, x ∈ E.

4.3 Mₙ-test (supremum test)

🔥 High — numericals
📜 TheoremLet \(f_n\to f\) pointwise on E and \(M_n=\sup_{x\in E}|f_n(x)-f(x)|\). Then \(f_n\to f\) uniformly on E iff \(M_n\to0\).
✒️ Proof
  1. If \(M_n\to0\): choose N with \(M_n\lt\varepsilon\) for n ≥ N; then \(|f_n(x)-f(x)|\le M_n\lt\varepsilon\) for all x. Uniform.
  2. If uniform: there is N with \(|f_n(x)-f(x)|\lt\frac\varepsilon2\) for all x, n ≥ N; taking sup, \(M_n\le\frac\varepsilon2\lt\varepsilon\). So \(M_n\to0\). ∎
🧠 Method (har numerical me)(1) Limit f(x) nikalo. (2) \(g(x)=|f_n(x)-f(x)|\). (3) g ka maximum (g′ = 0) \(=M_n\). (4) \(M_n\to0\) ⇒ uniform, warna nahi.
✍️ Solved example 1 Q. Show that \(f_n(x)=\frac{x}{1+nx^2}\) converges uniformly on ℝ.
f(x) = 0. \(g(x)=\frac{|x|}{1+nx^2}\); for x > 0, \(g'(x)=\frac{1-nx^2}{(1+nx^2)^2}=0\Rightarrow x=\frac1{\sqrt n}\). \(M_n=g(\frac1{\sqrt n})=\frac{1/\sqrt n}{2}=\frac1{2\sqrt n}\to0\). Uniform. ∎
✍️ Solved example 2 Q. Show that \(f_n(x)=x^n\) is not uniformly convergent on [0, 1] but is uniformly convergent on [0, k], 0 < k < 1.
Limit: f(x) = 0 for 0 ≤ x < 1, f(1) = 1. On [0, 1): \(\sup_{[0,1)}x^n=1\) (take x close to 1), so \(M_n=1\not\to0\): not uniform on [0, 1]. (Also each \(x^n\) is continuous but f is not — impossible under uniform convergence, by 4.6.)
On [0, k]: \(M_n=\sup x^n=k^n\to0\). Uniform. ∎
✍️ Solved example 3 Q. Test \(f_n(x)=\frac{nx}{1+n^2x^2}\) on [0, 1].
f(x) = 0. At \(x=\frac1n\), \(f_n=\frac12\); so \(M_n\ge\frac12\not\to0\): not uniform on [0, 1]. On [a, 1] (a > 0) for \(n\gt\frac1a\), \(f_n\) is decreasing in x there, so \(M_n=f_n(a)=\frac{na}{1+n^2a^2}\to0\): uniform. ∎

4.4 Weierstrass M-test

🔥 High
📜 TheoremIf \(|u_n(x)|\le M_n\) for all x ∈ E and all n, and \(\sum M_n\) converges, then \(\sum u_n(x)\) converges uniformly and absolutely on E.
✒️ Proof
  1. \(\sum M_n\) converges, so by the Cauchy criterion there is N with \(M_{n+1}+\dots+M_m\lt\varepsilon\) for m > n ≥ N.
  2. For every x ∈ E: \(|u_{n+1}(x)+\dots+u_m(x)|\le|u_{n+1}(x)|+\dots+|u_m(x)|\le M_{n+1}+\dots+M_m\lt\varepsilon\).
  3. N is independent of x, so by the Cauchy criterion (4.2) the series converges uniformly; the middle inequality shows absolute convergence too. ∎
✍️ Solved examples
  1. \(\sum\frac{\sin nx}{n^2}\) on ℝ: \(\big|\frac{\sin nx}{n^2}\big|\le\frac1{n^2}=M_n\), \(\sum\frac1{n^2}\) converges ⇒ uniform.
  2. \(\sum\frac{\cos nx}{n^p}\), p > 1, on ℝ: \(M_n=\frac1{n^p}\) ⇒ uniform.
  3. \(\sum\frac{1}{n^2+x^2}\) on ℝ: \(\le\frac1{n^2}\) ⇒ uniform.
  4. \(\sum x^n\) on [−r, r], 0 < r < 1: \(|x^n|\le r^n\), \(\sum r^n\) converges ⇒ uniform.
  5. \(\sum\frac{x}{n(1+nx^2)}\) on ℝ: \(\frac{|x|}{1+nx^2}\le\frac1{2\sqrt n}\) (Example 4.3-1), so each term \(\le\frac{1}{2n^{3/2}}\) ⇒ uniform.

4.5 Abel's and Dirichlet's tests for uniform convergence

⭐ Medium
📜 Dirichlet's testIf the partial sums \(U_n(x)\) of \(\sum u_n(x)\) are uniformly bounded on E (\(|U_n(x)|\le M\)), and for each x \(\{v_n(x)\}\) is monotonically decreasing with \(v_n\to0\) uniformly on E, then \(\sum u_nv_n\) converges uniformly on E.
✒️ ProofExactly as in 3.6: \(\big|\sum_{k=n+1}^mu_k(x)v_k(x)\big|\le2Mv_{n+1}(x)\). Uniform convergence of \(v_n\) to 0 gives N (independent of x) with \(2Mv_{n+1}(x)\lt\varepsilon\) for n ≥ N and all x. Apply the Cauchy criterion 4.2. ∎
📜 Abel's testIf \(\sum u_n(x)\) converges uniformly on E, and \(\{v_n(x)\}\) is monotonic in n for each x and uniformly bounded (\(|v_n(x)|\le K\)), then \(\sum u_nv_n\) converges uniformly on E.
✒️ ProofLet \(R_{n,p}(x)=u_{n+1}(x)+\dots+u_{n+p}(x)\). By uniform convergence, \(|R_{n,p}(x)|\lt\varepsilon\) for all p and x when n ≥ N. Partial summation on the block (with \(R_{n,p}\) as partial sums) gives \(\big|\sum_{k=n+1}^{n+p}u_kv_k\big|\le\varepsilon\big(\sum|v_k-v_{k+1}|+|v_{n+p}|\big)\). Monotonicity makes \(\sum|v_k-v_{k+1}|=|v_{n+1}-v_{n+p}|\le2K\), so the block is \(\le3K\varepsilon\). Cauchy criterion ⇒ uniform. ∎
✍️ Example\(\sum\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n+x^2}\) on ℝ: M-test fails (not absolutely convergent). \(u_n=(-1)^n\) has partial sums bounded by 1; \(v_n=\frac1{n+x^2}\) decreases in n and \(0\lt v_n\le\frac1n\to0\) uniformly. Dirichlet ⇒ uniform.

4.6 Uniform convergence and continuity, integration, differentiation

🔥 High
📜 Theorem 1 (Continuity)If each \(f_n\) is continuous on E and \(f_n\to f\) uniformly on E, then f is continuous on E.
✒️ Proof
  1. Let \(x_0\in E\), ε > 0. Choose N with \(|f_N(x)-f(x)|\lt\frac\varepsilon3\) for all x ∈ E.
  2. \(f_N\) is continuous at \(x_0\): there is δ with \(|f_N(x)-f_N(x_0)|\lt\frac\varepsilon3\) for \(|x-x_0|\lt\delta\).
  3. For such x:
$$|f(x)-f(x_0)|\le|f(x)-f_N(x)|+|f_N(x)-f_N(x_0)|+|f_N(x_0)-f(x_0)|\lt\varepsilon .$$ Hence f is continuous at \(x_0\). ∎
Series: the sum of a uniformly convergent series of continuous functions is continuous.
📜 Theorem 2 (Integration)If \(f_n\in\mathcal R\) on [a, b] and \(f_n\to f\) uniformly on [a, b], then \(f\in\mathcal R\) and \(\displaystyle\int_a^bf\,dx=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_a^bf_n\,dx\).
✒️ Proof
  1. Let \(\varepsilon_n=\sup_{[a,b]}|f_n-f|\to0\). Then \(f_n-\varepsilon_n\le f\le f_n+\varepsilon_n\).
  2. So \(\int(f_n-\varepsilon_n)\le\underline\int f\le\overline\int f\le\int(f_n+\varepsilon_n)\), giving \(0\le\overline\int f-\underline\int f\le2\varepsilon_n(b-a)\to0\). Hence \(f\in\mathcal R\).
  3. \(\big|\int f-\int f_n\big|\le\varepsilon_n(b-a)\to0\). ∎
Series: a uniformly convergent series of integrable functions can be integrated term by term.
📜 Theorem 3 (Differentiation)Let \(\{f_n\}\) be differentiable on [a, b], \(\{f_n(x_0)\}\) converge for some \(x_0\in[a,b]\), and \(\{f_n'\}\) converge uniformly on [a, b]. Then \(\{f_n\}\) converges uniformly to some f, and \(f'(x)=\lim f_n'(x)\) for all x ∈ [a, b].
✒️ Proof
  1. Given ε, choose N such that for m, n ≥ N: \(|f_n(x_0)-f_m(x_0)|\lt\frac\varepsilon2\) and \(|f_n'(t)-f_m'(t)|\lt\frac{\varepsilon}{2(b-a)}\) for all t.
  2. MVT applied to \(f_n-f_m\): \(|[f_n(x)-f_m(x)]-[f_n(t)-f_m(t)]|\le\frac{|x-t|\varepsilon}{2(b-a)}\le\frac\varepsilon2\) for all x, t.
  3. With t = x₀: \(|f_n(x)-f_m(x)|\lt\varepsilon\) for all x. By 4.2, \(f_n\to f\) uniformly.
  4. Fix x and set \(\varphi_n(t)=\frac{f_n(t)-f_n(x)}{t-x}\), \(\varphi(t)=\frac{f(t)-f(x)}{t-x}\) (t ≠ x). Step 2 gives \(|\varphi_n(t)-\varphi_m(t)|\le\frac{\varepsilon}{2(b-a)}\), so \(\varphi_n\to\varphi\) uniformly; and \(\lim_{t\to x}\varphi_n(t)=f_n'(x)\).
  5. Uniform convergence lets us interchange the limits: \(\lim_{t\to x}\varphi(t)=\lim_{n}f_n'(x)\), i.e. \(f'(x)=\lim f_n'(x)\). ∎
✍️ Counter-example (why \(f_n'\) must converge uniformly)\(f_n(x)=\frac{\sin nx}{\sqrt n}\): \(|f_n|\le\frac1{\sqrt n}\), so \(f_n\to0\) uniformly and f′ = 0. But \(f_n'(x)=\sqrt n\cos nx\), and \(f_n'(0)=\sqrt n\to\infty\).
